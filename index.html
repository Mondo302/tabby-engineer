Why was I declined?

It’s a design project.

I noticed something in buy-now-pay-later apps. They can decline a purchase and never say why. I redesigned that moment.

The fix in one sentence: when a purchase is declined, say why, say when it will be looked at again, and offer one thing the person can do.

It’s an independent concept of mine. It isn’t made by, or affiliated with, Tabby.

She never paid late. Tabby still shut her out for months.

Someone I know used Tabby for a SAR 1,000 plane ticket.

She paid it.

She isn’t the type to owe anyone. She pays upfront whenever she can, because she hates having a payment hanging over her.

Then her payments started getting declined.

Not once. For months.

She contacted support. They told her they didn’t know why either.

Tabby’s message to her: try again in a few months.

She did. One day it just worked again.

Nobody told her why it stopped.

Nobody told her it was fixed.

As she described it to me. Her account, not a Tabby statement.

It isn’t her account. It’s how it’s built.

I wanted to know if it was just her.

So I read Tabby’s own help centre.

About a decline, it says each order is reviewed on its own, your limit can vary by store, and support can’t change the decision. It recommends trying again.

I asked someone else.

He tried to buy a phone. 2,000, declined. 3,000, declined. 4,000, declined. 5,000, declined. 1,800 went through, for 3 months only.

The only explanation he got came from his uncle.

Not from the app.

Then I counted. About 9 of the 28 Trustpilot reviews I could see were about a decline or an unexplained limit. Roughly 32%. People who write reviews skew angry, so that’s a signal, not a headcount.

Then I opened the app.

Four tabs. Home, Shop, Payments, Profile.

None of them shows your spending limit.

Payments tells you exactly what you owe.

Nothing tells you what you can spend.

The number that decides whether your purchase goes through is the one number the app never shows you.

So I redesigned the one moment that failed her.

Today

  1. Purchase declined
  2. The app gives no reason, no date and no next step
  3. A generic help article: try again
  4. Guesswork, or a relative’s theory
  5. Trust drops. No way back to a higher limit.

Proposed

  1. Purchase declined
  2. Same screen: the reason, the next review and one next step
  3. The person acts: a smaller amount, another plan, an instalment paid
  4. The purchase completes, or they wait knowing what comes next
  5. On-time payments build the history a higher limit needs.

Now imagine the app had told her.

Why her payments were declined.

When it would be looked at again.

Your limit was reviewed and restored.

This isn’t a decline problem. It’s a silence problem.

Say why. Say when it will be looked at again. Say when it’s over.

Three people, one gap

Two are people I spoke to. The third is a pattern in public reviews. None of it is a Tabby statement.

The never-late payer

What happened
Pays upfront, never late. Declined for months. Support couldn’t explain why.
What was missing
No reason, no review date, and nobody told her when it was fixed.
What the screen says instead
It’s a review. Here’s the date. Nothing is needed from you. We’ll tell you when it lifts.

The new account

What happened
Tried 2,000, 3,000, 4,000 and 5,000: all declined. 1,800 went through, for 3 months only.
What was missing
The app never said the starting limit is low, and offered no choice of plan length.
What the screen says instead
Your account is new, so your limit starts lower. Here’s what fits: a smaller amount, or another plan length.

The limit cut

What happened
A long-standing account, reviewed, and its limit reduced without warning.
What was missing
No notice of the cut, no reason, and no date for the next review.
What the screen says instead
A review lowered your limit. The next one is on this date. Here’s what counts toward it.

What I chose not to build

  • A real Tabby connection. This is a static demonstration with realistic mock data: no backend, no accounts, no database.
  • Fixes for cashback, cards, refunds or human support. Those complaints are real. I left them out to keep one idea sharp.
  • Exact decline rules, or a promise of a higher limit. The fix makes a decision legible. Saudi regulation caps how much a person can owe through buy-now-pay-later, so it can’t promise growth.

How I’d run it: show the new decline screen to half of new and recently reviewed accounts, keep today’s screen for the other half, and compare these three numbers after a fixed period. I’m not predicting the results.

What ships first

One screen: the decline screen. Add a reason category, the date of the next review and one next step, and nothing else.

The limit screen, the “restored” message and the plan-length alternatives come after, and each one has to earn its place against the three numbers above.
